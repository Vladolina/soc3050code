# Drone Attitude: Flying a Quadcopter That Does Not Exist
## SOC3050 ARM Edition — Part 3, Applications

**Reference**: [STM32 Reference Manual](https://www.st.com/resource/en/reference_manual/rm0490-stm32c0x1-advanced-armbased-32bit-mcus-stmicroelectronics.pdf) ·
**Board manual**: [UM2953, STM32 Nucleo-64 boards (MB1717)](https://www.st.com/resource/en/user_manual/um2953-stm32c0-nucleo64-board-mb1717-stmicroelectronics.pdf)

**A quadcopter flies on a 48 MHz chip with no FPU — and you tune it by
turning a knob.**

Press A: four motor bars spool up on the OLED and an artificial horizon
settles level. Push the joystick: the horizon tilts 20° in a third of a
second. Press B: a gust hits it at 229°/s and it is back within 3° in 0.17 s.
Turn the knob too far: it buzzes. Too little: it wobbles itself into the
ground. None of it needs a drone — the drone is a physics model running on
the same chip, in its own task, and your flight controller cannot tell. On
the PC, the station draws it in 3D and times you through a course of rings.

**Never flown a drone? Start at slide 0a.** Part 0 explains, with no
equations beyond `a = g·tan θ`, how four propellers hold a drone up, turn
it, and move it — and why it needs a computer at all.

Figures marked **measured on the host** come from `host/sitl.c`, which links
this lesson's own `world.c` and `control.c` into a PC program. Cycle counts
come from lesson 10's Cortex-M0+ model running this lesson's `Main.elf`.
The lesson has been watched running in Wokwi with the station (2026-11-08);
the numbers on the slides are still the host's.

## The Board: This Lesson's Pins

Before any register, find the pins. Every highlighted dot is a header pin that
this lesson's `diagram.json` wires something to — the same circuit you paste
into Wokwi — at its position on the Nucleo-C031C6, to scale. A label reads
*MCU pin (header name, if different)* and what the pin does here.

The two outer double rows are ST's **morpho** headers, which reach every pin
of the chip; the two inner single rows are the **Arduino**-compatible header
(A0–A5 on the left, D0–D15 on the right). Some MCU pins appear on both — PA5 is
also `D13` — so the highlighted position is the one this lesson's wire uses.
The real board's headers are drawn in its manual, UM2953 (top bar).

`GPIOA->IDR`, bits 2*n*+1 : 2*n* of `GPIOA->MODER`, and so on (lesson 05,
slide 4). Each cell below is one pin, drawn in register order, marked with how
this lesson configures it.

## Slide 0a: Part 0 — How a Drone Flies (No Drone Experience Needed)

Before any code: what a quadcopter **is**, why it stays up, and how it moves.
Everything later in this lesson is one of these ideas turned into C.

| Slide | Question | The one-line answer |
|---|---|---|
| 0b | What is it made of? | a frame, four motors with propellers, a battery, and a small computer |
| 0c | How does a propeller lift? | it throws air down; the air pushes it up |
| 0d | How does it hover? | the four thrusts add up to the weight |
| 0e | Why do two propellers spin backwards? | so the frame itself does not spin |
| 0f | How does it roll, pitch and yaw? | by making some motors faster and others slower |
| 0g | How does it fly *somewhere*? | it tilts, and the tilted thrust pushes it sideways |
| 0h | Why can no human fly it unaided? | nothing holds it level: it must be corrected 250 times a second |
| 0i | What words will we use? | a glossary |
| 0j | What comes next? | a map of the rest of the deck |

A quadcopter has **no rudder, no flaps, no wings and no gearbox.** The only
thing it can change is how fast each of its four motors spins. Every
manoeuvre you will ever see a drone make — hover, climb, turn, flip, race —
is four numbers going up and down. This lesson writes the program that
chooses those four numbers.

```svg
  <text x="160" y="30" class="hi">thrust T</text>

  <path class="dash" d="M115 110 V196" marker-end="url(#d13q)"/>

  <text x="310" y="40" class="mono">T = k · ω²</text>
  <text x="310" y="64" class="lbl">ω = rotor speed. Twice the speed, four times the thrust.</text>
  <text x="310" y="100" class="mono">fixed blades</text>

  <text x="310" y="160" class="mono">one direction</text>
  <text x="310" y="184" class="lbl">It cannot push down. Zero is the least it can do.</text>

```

Newton's third law: the propeller pushes a column of air **down**, so the air
pushes the propeller **up**. That push is **thrust**.

Three facts every later slide depends on:

- **Speed is the only control.** A helicopter tilts its blades; a cheap
  quadcopter cannot. To change thrust it must change the motor speed.
- **Speed cannot change instantly.** The rotor has inertia: it takes time to
  spin up. In `world.c` each motor reaches 63 % of a new command after
  **30 ms** — the *motor lag*, and slide 19's buzzing comes from it.
- **Thrust has limits:** 0 (stopped) to full (4 N here). A motor cannot
  push down, and cannot exceed full. Slide 16 is about running into them.

Real thrust grows with the square of speed. `world.c` makes thrust simply
proportional to the command, as if the ESC had already undone the square —
many flight controllers do exactly that correction.

To **hover**, the four thrusts together must equal the drone's weight. More
and it climbs, less and it sinks. That shared level is the **throttle**: all
four motors up together, or all four down.

This lesson's drone weighs 0.5 kg, so each motor needs 1.23 N — **31 %** of
its range. The station's motor diagram shows numbers near that, and the
pilot task ramps the throttle to 0.35 when you arm.

**Why the spare thrust matters:** every correction later in this lesson is
"this motor a bit more, that one a bit less". A motor at 31 % can go **down
by 31 %** and **up by 69 %** before it hits a limit. A drone with no spare
thrust can hover but cannot steer.

A spinning propeller drags on the air, and the air drags back. The motor
feels that as a **twist**: it pushes the propeller one way, so the propeller
pushes the motor — and the whole frame — the **other** way. A helicopter
needs a tail rotor to stop this; a quadcopter uses its own propellers.

Two propellers spin **clockwise** (M0, M2) and two **counter-clockwise**
(M1, M3), on the diagonals. At equal speeds their twists cancel exactly and
the drone does not turn. In `world.c` each motor's twist is
**0.016 m × its thrust** — about 0.02 N·m at hover, four of them, summing to
zero.

Keep this in mind: the cancellation is only exact while the pairs are
**equal**. Unbalance them on purpose and the drone turns — that is yaw, on
the next slide.

## Slide 0g: To Go Somewhere, It Tilts

A quadcopter's thrust always points **straight out of its top**. It has no
propeller that pushes sideways. To move forward it **tilts forward**, and
part of the thrust now points backward-and-down at the air — pushing the
drone forward.

## Slide 0h: Nothing Holds It Level — Why a Computer Flies It

An aeroplane is **stable**: let go of the controls and its wings and tail
bring it back to level flight. A quadcopter has nothing like that. Spin it
and it keeps spinning; tilt it and it stays tilted — and, by slide 0g,
starts sliding sideways.

And it is always being tipped. **One motor 1 % stronger than the rest** —
normal manufacturing spread — pushes 0.04 N harder at 0.085 m from the
centre. By slide 4's numbers that is an angular acceleration of
0.85 rad/s², and left alone it tips the drone over **20° in one second**.
Add gusts, a battery that is not quite centred, and a bent propeller.

A human reacts in about 0.2 s. So the flight controller runs a **feedback
loop**, 250 times a second here:

## Slide 1d: The Whole Program — What `main()` Does First

Before any task runs, `main()` (the bottom of `Main.c`) brings the board up
in an order where each step can rely on the ones before it:

| Order | Call | Why first |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | `uart_init`, banner | so every later step can report itself |
| 2 | `pad_init`, `beep_init` | the joystick, knob, buttons and buzzer |
| 3 | `i2c_init`, `oled_init`, `mpu_init` | one bus, two chips; a missing MPU6050 just means SIM mode only |
| 4 | `world_init`, `world_sense`, `control_init` | the drone level and still; a first IMU sample ready |
| 5 | `kbd_init`, two queues, five `os_task_create` | the station's keys, the beat, the tasks |
| 6 | `os_start()` | never returns: from here the RTOS decides who runs |

After `os_start()` there is no main loop. Everything the program does from
then on happens inside one of the five tasks of slide 1c — the shape every
RTOS program has (lesson 07).

## Slide 2: The Contract

`control.h` is the whole interface. Everything the flight controller may know:

```c
typedef struct {
    float    gyro[3];       /* body rates p, q, r                 rad/s      */
    float    accel[3];      /* specific force, body frame         m/s^2      */
    uint32_t seq;           /* +1 per new IMU sample; unchanged = stale      */
    uint8_t  imu_ok;        /* 0 = the driver says the IMU failed            */
    /* the pilot - a radio receiver is a sensor too, from here */
    float    rc_roll;       /* roll  setpoint                     rad        */
    float    rc_pitch;      /* pitch setpoint                     rad        */
    float    rc_yaw_rate;   /* yaw-rate setpoint                  rad/s      */
    float    rc_throttle;   /* collective thrust                  0..1       */
    uint8_t  rc_arm;        /* arm switch                         0/1        */
} sensors_t;

typedef struct {
    float    motor[4];      /* M0..M3 command, 0..1 (0 = stopped)            */
    uint8_t  armed;
} actuators_t;

void control_step(const sensors_t *in, actuators_t *out);
```

Three design choices worth copying:

- **No clock in the interface.** `control_step()` is called at exactly
  `CTRL_HZ` (250) and counts on it — so the host test flies about four
  minutes of simulated flight in about one second (measured), and gets the
  answers the chip would get in four minutes.
- **`seq` makes staleness visible.** A frozen driver that keeps returning the
  last reading looks perfectly healthy — unless every sample carries a count.
- **The pilot is an input.** A radio receiver is just another sensor.

```svg
  <text x="330" y="110" class="mono">yaw   + : nose to the right (r)</text>
  <text x="330" y="150" class="lbl">Left motors (shaded) push harder: roll +.</text>
  <text x="330" y="170" class="lbl">Front motors push harder: pitch +.</text>

```

Level and still, the accelerometer reads **(0, 0, −9.81)**. It measures the
force *holding it up* — specific force — which points up, and up is −*z*.
Get one of these signs wrong and the controller pushes the wrong way. Lab
Part 6 does that on purpose.

## Slide 4: The Plant — a Quadcopter on a Gimbal

`world.c` is a rigid body that **rotates freely about its centre of mass** —
the classic attitude test stand. It cannot climb or drift; position is
lesson 14's.

| Parameter | Value | Why that number |
|---|---|---|
| mass, arm | 0.5 kg, 0.12 m | a 250-class quad |
| inertia *Ixx, Iyy, Izz* | 0.004, 0.004, 0.007 kg·m² | yaw is hardest to turn |
| thrust per motor | 0–4 N, linear in command | thrust/weight 3.3 |
| motor lag | first order, τ = 30 ms | props have inertia |
| drag torque | 0.016 m × thrust | the only thing that yaws a quad |
| rate | 500 Hz (`WORLD_DT` = 2 ms) | twice the controller |

Each step: motors lag toward their clamped commands; the X geometry turns
four thrusts into three torques; **Euler's equations** turn torques into
angular acceleration,

```
I w' = tau - w x (I w) - c w
```

and a **quaternion** integrates the rates, so no attitude is singular — a
flipped drone is just upside down, not a division by zero.

In words: Euler's equation is Newton's *F = m·a* for turning — **torque =
inertia × angular acceleration**. More torque spins it up faster; a heavier
or wider drone (larger *I*) spins up slower. The extra terms are a
spinning body's gyroscopic coupling and a little air damping. A quaternion
is four numbers that store "which way the drone is pointing" without the
trouble three angles have when it points straight up. Past 90° of tilt
the world latches `crashed`.

---

## Slide 5: Four Thrusts, Three Torques

With each motor at (±*d*, ±*d*), *d* = arm/√2 = 0.085 m, straight from
`world_step()`:

```c
float tx = d * (T[0] + T[3] - T[1] - T[2]);              /* roll  */
float ty = d * (T[0] + T[1] - T[2] - T[3]);              /* pitch */
float tz = w->k_drag * (T[1] + T[3] - T[0] - T[2]);      /* yaw   */
```

What it means for control: a differential command of *u* on the four motors
(±*u* each) gives a roll acceleration of

```
d * 4 * (u * 4 N) / Ixx = 0.085 * 16 / 0.004 * u  =  339 u   rad/s^2
```

So a 10 % differential (*u* = 0.1) spins the drone up at 34 rad/s² — about
1 900 °/s². Strong, but **30 ms late** (the motor lag) and **clipped at
0 and 1**: a motor cannot spin backwards, and cannot spin faster than full.
Both facts come back on slides 16 and 19.

## Slide 6: The Simulated IMU — Built to Be Annoying

`world_sense()` produces what an MPU6050 bolted to the frame would report:

| Imperfection | In `world.c` | What it does to you |
|---|---|---|
| gyro **bias** | +0.8, −0.6, +0.4 °/s, plus a slow random walk | integrated, it drifts without limit |
| gyro noise | 0.3 °/s, 1σ | small |
| accel noise | 0.3 m/s² (0.03 g), 1σ | small |
| **motor vibration** | 6 m/s² × mean motor: ~0.19 g at hover | tilt from accel jumps by degrees |
| **low-pass filter** | first order, 44 Hz (the chip's `DLPF_CFG = 3`) | smooth — and ~4 ms **late** |

Gravity in the body frame is the third row of the rotation matrix; the
accelerometer reads minus it. The noise is a seeded xorshift generator, so a
host run repeats bit for bit — a test that fails "sometimes" is worth
nothing.

**Why a gimbal, honestly.** In free flight an accelerometer at the centre of
mass mostly measures *thrust*, not gravity: a tilted drone accelerating
sideways can read straight "down". Real filters still work because of drag
and because on average the drone is not accelerating — a subtlety this
model avoids by holding the centre of mass still.

## Slide 7: Tilt From Gravity — Right on Average, Noisy Every Sample

Gravity is a fixed arrow in the world. Seen in the body frame it leans:

```c
s.roll_acc  = f_atan2(-ay, -az);                       /* from gravity  */
s.pitch_acc = f_atan2(ax, f_sqrt(ay * ay + az * az));
```

`f_atan2` and `f_sqrt` are `fmath.h`'s — a dozen multiplies each, instead of
libm's ~4 KB.

The accelerometer **never drifts**: gravity does not move. But it sees every
other acceleration too, and on a quad that means the motors' shake: 0.19 g of
vibration is an 11° wobble in a single sample.

**Measured on the host** (slide 11): flying on the accelerometer alone
(`alpha = 0`), the estimate is off by **3.2° RMS, 7.2° worst**, and jumps
**6.0° from one 4 ms step to the next.**

## Slide 8: Tilt From the Gyro — Smooth, and Wrong by Tomorrow

A gyro measures rate. Integrate it:

```
roll(t + dt) = roll(t) + p * dt          (small angles: roll' = p)
```

No vibration problem: rate sensors barely notice linear shaking. But every
error is integrated too, and a **constant bias integrates into a ramp**:
0.8 °/s is 48° a minute.

**Measured on the host** (`alpha = 1`, gyro only): after 30 s the estimate is
**14.5° RMS from the truth, 26° at worst**. And the drone? It flew "level" —
level *according to its estimate* — while the true attitude leaned 26° and
climbing.

The failsafe did not fire. It watches the **estimate**, and the estimate
said 0°. *A failsafe is only as good as the number it checks.*

```c
float a = t->alpha;

```

The crossover is a time constant: **τ = α·dt / (1 − α)**. At α = 0.98 and
dt = 4 ms, τ = 0.196 s. Faster than τ, trust the gyro; slower, trust gravity.

## Slide 10: What a Gyro Bias Costs, Predicted

In steady state the filter's output stops moving, so the gyro's push and the
accelerometer's pull must balance. Work it through and the leftover error is

```
error = bias × τ  =  0.8 °/s × 0.196 s  =  0.16°
```

— a **constant**, not a ramp. That is the whole point: the accelerometer
bounds the drift the gyro would otherwise accumulate forever.

And the trade is now visible as one number:

- **bigger τ** (α → 1): less vibration gets through, more bias error stays;
- **smaller τ** (α → 0): bias error shrinks, vibration noise grows.

Measured on the next slide: at α = 0.98 the true error is **0.20° RMS** —
the predicted 0.16° plus the noise that leaks through.

The **outer** loop turns an angle error into a *rate* request: 10° off asks

how ArduPilot, PX4 and Betaflight's angle mode are built.

**The rule:** the inner loop must be several times faster than the outer.
Slide 19 shows what happens when it is not.

## Slide 13: The Rate PID, Line by Line

```c
float d = -(meas - *meas_prev) * CTRL_HZ;
*meas_prev = meas;
*dfilt += 0.5f * (d - *dfilt);

float u_free = k * t->rate_kp * e + t->rate_kd * *dfilt + *integ;
float u = f_clamp(u_free, -t->max_out, t->max_out);
```

| Term | Gain | Job |
|---|---|---|
| P | 0.12 × knob | 1 rad/s off → 12 % differential thrust |
| I | 0.15 | trims out what P cannot: a motor slightly weaker than the rest |
| D | 0.0008, on the **measurement**, low-passed | damping without a "derivative kick" when the setpoint steps |
| clamp | ±0.25 | no axis may take more than a quarter of the motor range |

D on the measurement rather than the error: when the stick jumps, the error
jumps, and d(error)/dt is a spike that would slam the motors. The
measurement never jumps — it is a body with inertia.

## Slide 14: Anti-Windup

Hold the drone level by hand while the stick asks for 30° of roll. The
error never shrinks, so the integrator **grows without limit**. Let go, and
all that stored "push harder" comes out at once.

```c
if (!t->anti_windup) {
    *integ += t->rate_ki * e * CTRL_DT;
} else if (u == u_free && !motors_saturated) {
    *integ = f_clamp(*integ + t->rate_ki * e * CTRL_DT, -t->i_limit, t->i_limit);
}
```

Stop integrating while the output is pinned — more integral cannot help,
it can only be unwound later. **Measured on the host** (`world.locked` holds
it for 2 s, then lets go — the shell's `hold` does the same in Wokwi):

| anti-windup | peak roll | within 2° of 30° by | end state |
|---|---|---|---|
| on | 29.8° | 0.28 s | flying |
| **off** | **179.5°** | never | **flipped; tilt failsafe** |

One `if`. The difference between a hover and an upside-down drone. The
controller also zeroes every integrator while disarmed or at zero throttle:
an integrator that winds up on the grass flips the drone on take-off.

## Slide 15: The X Mixer

Three torque commands and a throttle in, four motors out — the inverse of
slide 5:

```c
m[0] = th + r + u_p - u_y;      /* FL, CW   */
m[1] = th - r + u_p + u_y;      /* FR, CCW  */
m[2] = th - r - u_p - u_y;      /* RR, CW   */
m[3] = th + r - u_p + u_y;      /* RL, CCW  */
```

Check one column against the geometry: roll + needs the **left** motors
(M0, M3) up and the right ones down — `+r, -r, -r, +r`. ✓

Flip that one sign (`mix 1` → `mix -1` in the shell, or
`ctrl_tune.mix_roll_sign`) and every correction becomes a push the wrong
way. **Measured on the host**, flipped in hover: **tilt failsafe after
0.36 s, then a crash.** Positive feedback does not drift — it runs away
exponentially. Every first flight of a real drone starts with checking these
signs by hand, props off.

## Slide 16: Saturation — Asking for More Than Exists

A motor cannot do −10 % or 130 %. Three clamps, three different reasons:

| Clamp | Where | Why |
|---|---|---|
| rate setpoint ±200 °/s | angle loop | a 60° stick error must not ask for 540 °/s |
| axis output ±0.25 | rate loop | roll must not starve pitch of motor range |
| motor 0..1 | after the mixer | physics; and it raises `saturated` |

`saturated` feeds back into the anti-windup on the next step, so an
integrator never winds against a motor that is already at its limit. At
hover (throttle 0.35) a motor can go down by at most 0.35 before it stops —
asymmetric authority, one reason real drones hover near half throttle.

## Slide 17: When Not to Fly

Most of a real flight controller is rules. `control_step()` applies these
every 4 ms:

| Rule | Threshold | Why |
|---|---|---|
| arm only with **throttle low** | < 0.05 | a drone that arms at half throttle leaps at your face |
| arm only after **0.5 s of good samples** | `CTRL_SETTLE_STEPS` 125 | right after reset the estimate has not caught up |
| arm only when **level** | < 25° | not upside down in the grass |
| **disarm** past 60° tilt | `CTRL_TILT_LIMIT` | it is crashing; stop the props |
| **disarm** on a non-finite or absurd reading | NaN, > 2000 °/s, > 16 g | a broken IMU must not fly |
| **disarm** if `seq` freezes | 5 steps = 20 ms | a dead driver returns the last value forever |
| re-arm only after the switch is **cycled** | | a failsafe must not re-arm itself |

The settle rule was found by the host test: the first version armed a drone
held at 30°, because the estimate and the sensor's filter both still said
"level" a few milliseconds after reset.

## Slide 19: Too Little, Too Much — the Knob

The knob multiplies the rate loop's Kp, logarithmically: each eighth of a
turn doubles it, ×0.125 to ×8. **Measured on the host**, 20° roll step:

| Kp scale | rise | overshoot | settle (±1°) | rate RMS after 2 s | |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| ×0.125 | 0.16 s | **194 %** | never | **235 °/s** | swings to 59°, 1° short of the failsafe |
| ×0.25 | 0.13 s | 44.5 % | 2.17 s | 7.2 °/s | sloppy |
| ×0.5 | 0.12 s | 13.5 % | 0.57 s | 1.0 °/s | |
| **×1** | 0.20 s | 0.3 % | **0.34 s** | 1.1 °/s | default |
| ×2 | 0.20 s | 0.4 % | 0.35 s | 1.0 °/s | |
| ×4 | 0.23 s | 0.4 % | 0.37 s | 1.1 °/s | |
| ×8 | 0.19 s | 2.1 % | 0.37 s | **19.2 °/s** | **buzzes** |

**Too low** is not "slow": when the inner loop is slower than the outer
(slide 12) the cascade **oscillates** — the outer loop asks, the inner
cannot deliver in time, the outer asks harder. **Too high** buzzes: the loop is full of lag —
the sensor's 44 Hz filter, 4 ms of sampling and computing, the motors' 30 ms
— and at ×8 the loop is fast enough that those lags add up to half a cycle
of the oscillation, where a correction arrives just in time to push the
wrong way. The ±0.25 clamp keeps the buzz small — on a real
quad it is the hot-motor whine every pilot learns to hear.

## Slide 20: Five Tasks on One Core

| Task | Rate | Priority | Does |
|---|---|---|---|
| world | 500 Hz | 5 (most urgent) | `world_step` + `world_sense` → `g_sens` |
| ctrl | 250 Hz (every 2nd world step) | 4 | `g_sens` → `control_step` → `g_act` |
| pilot | 50 Hz | 3 | joystick, buttons, knob, throttle ramp, `$ATT` at 25 Hz, beeps |
| shell | on input | 2 | `stats`, `alpha`, `aw`, `mix`, `kick`, `hold`, `quiet`, `loud`; the station's `$KEY` / `$MIRROR` |
| disp | 15 Hz | 1 | horizon, motor bars, flush to the OLED, then up to 2 `$FB` mirror frames |

The physics is the most urgent task. It waits for one thing only: every
second step it hands ctrl a beat (a one-item queue) and waits until ctrl's
step is done, so the controller sees every fourth millisecond of flight
exactly once even on a CPU too slow to keep up. If a cycle overruns its
2 ms, the world then sleeps as long as the cycle took, so the lower tasks
still run and the flight goes into slow motion (Wokwi does this). The
structs cross between tasks inside two-line critical sections (lesson 03's
tearing):

```c
__disable_irq(); in = g_sens; __enable_irq();
```

A whole `sensors_t` is 52 bytes — a torn copy would pair this step's gyro
with last step's accelerometer.

## Slide 21: Counting Cycles With SysTick

The kernel owns SysTick at 1 kHz: `LOAD` = 47 999, and `VAL` counts **down**
at 48 MHz.

```regs
# SysTick, 0xE000E010 - the kernel set it up; we only read VAL
LOAD ; SYST_RVR, 47999 here | 32 | !23:0 RELOAD
VAL ; SYST_CVR, counts down | 32 | !23:0 CURRENT
```

Read `VAL` before and after; the difference is cycles, if the code takes less
than one tick (one wrap at most):

```c
static inline uint32_t cyc_elapsed(uint32_t v0, uint32_t v1)
{
    return v0 >= v1 ? v0 - v1 : v0 + (SysTick->LOAD + 1u) - v1;
}
```

Interrupts are **off** for the measurement, so neither the tick nor another
task lands in the middle — which is why only **one step in 64** is timed:
holding interrupts off for ~0.7 ms costs received UART bytes if done often.
Type `stats` and the firmware prints its own numbers. Lesson 10 builds the
same instrument.

## Slide 23: The Artificial Horizon

The left half of the OLED is a pilot's attitude indicator:

```c
float s = f_sin(roll), c = f_cos(roll);
float slope = s / c, cy = 32.0f + pitch * DEG;
for (int x = 1; x < 63; x++) {
    int y = (int)(cy - ((float)x - 32.0f) * slope);
    oled_vline(x, y, 63 - y, OLED_ON);     /* fill the ground */
}
```

Roll right and the **horizon** tilts the other way — its right end rises;
pitch up and it drops, 1 pixel per degree. The aircraft symbol is drawn with
XOR so it shows over sky and ground. In SIM mode a **dotted line** is the
*true* horizon from the world — what no real pilot ever gets to see. The gap
between the two lines is the estimator's error, live.

The right half: mode and state, four motor bars laid out as seen from above
(FL FR / RL RR), roll and pitch, the knob's Kp, alpha, and the measured loop
rate and frame rate.

## Slide 23b: The Station — Fly It in 3D, Let the Clock Grade You

`uv run tools/station/station.py` (VS Code task **ARM: Station**) turns the
`$ATT` frames into a 3D view of the drone and a race through ten rings:

- the quad drawn at the **true** attitude, each propeller disc lit by its
  motor's thrust — slide 0f's table, live;
- a white wireframe at the attitude the **estimator** believes: when it
  separates from the solid quad, that is slide 11's error;
- press `a` to arm and the clock starts; fly the rings in order with the
  arrows; a trail and a map show the path you flew.

**Who does what** — the line between the two is the honest part:

| Done by | What |
|---|---|
| **the board** | the attitude: physics, sensor, filter, both loops, mixer, failsafes |
| **the station** | turning tilt into travel with slide 0g's `a = g·tan θ`, minus air drag (0.7 /s), at ring height |

The board's drone is on a gimbal (slide 4): it tilts but cannot travel. The
station does the travel arithmetic — on the board's own clock — and says so
on screen. Everything that decides your lap time is still the board's: how
fast the attitude follows the stick, whether it overshoots, how wrong the
estimate is. At 20° the drone tops out near **5 m/s** (3.57 / 0.7).

**So a lap time is a measurement.** Fly the course at `1` (Kp ×0.19), `3`
(×1) and `5` (×5.3); fly it with `alpha 0` and with `alpha 1000`; fly it
after `b` throws a gust at you. The class tasks in the README become a
league table.

## Slide 24: The Real Sensor — MPU Mode

Joystick **SEL** swaps the world for the Wokwi MPU6050 at `0x68`, on the
OLED's I²C wires. Same `control_step()`, same estimator; `rc_arm` is forced
to 0 — **nothing flies on a slider**.

```regs
# MPU6050 registers (layout from the Adafruit_MPU6050 driver)
CONFIG ; 0x1A, = 3: 44 Hz | 8 = 0x03 | !2:0 DLPF_CFG
GYRO_CONFIG ; 0x1B, left at 0: 250 deg/s | 8 | 4:3 FS_SEL
PWR_MGMT_1 ; 0x6B, = 0: awake | 8 = 0x00 | !6 SLEEP
```

One 14-byte read from `0x3B` gets accel X, Y, Z, temperature, gyro X, Y, Z,
big-endian. At the reset ranges, 16 384 LSB per g and 131 LSB per °/s. The
chip is taken as mounted X forward, **Y left**, Z up, so body *y* = −chip Y
and body *z* = −chip Z.

**One bus, two tasks.** The display holds the bus mutex for a whole flush —
about 1 KB, ~25 ms at 400 kHz. In MPU mode the control task waits behind
it, then catches up with several back-to-back reads (`stats` counts late
wake-ups). Fine for an estimator lab; on a real drone the IMU gets **its own
SPI bus** for exactly this reason.

## Slide 25: Telemetry for the Host

Every 40 ms, a lesson 08 frame with its checksum:

```
$ATT,<ms>,<roll>,<pitch>,<roll set>,<pitch set>,<true roll>,<true pitch>,<M0>,<M1>,<M2>,<M3>,<armed>,<Kp x100>*CS
```

Angles in tenths of a degree, motors in thousandths — integers, because
`printf` here has no float support. In MPU mode it is

```
uv run ..\08_UART_And_Python_Host\host.py --file cap.txt --chart ATT,2,-300,300
```

## Slide 26: What Was Measured — and What Could Not Be

**Measured on the host** (`host/run.bat`, 24 checks, all pass): step
responses (roll rise 0.20 s, settle 0.34 s, overshoot 0.3 %), a gust of
229 °/s rejected in 0.17 s with an 11° peak, all six failsafe cases, the
alpha table, the gain sweep, anti-windup on and off, and the flipped mixer.
The same `world.c` and `control.c` compile into the firmware unchanged.

**Measured by lesson 10's M0+ model** (`host/cycles.py`): the cycle
budget — 48.8 % of the CPU for world plus controller at 1 wait state.

**Watched in Wokwi, not measured there:** the lesson runs — OLED, sound,
buttons, the station's 3D race — after a fix: Wokwi's CPU runs slower than
its clock, so the world task overran its 2 ms and starved every other task
until it was given an overrun guard and a lock-step with the controller
(slide 20). Not yet read from Wokwi: what `stats` prints, the MPU6050's
axes. The M0+ model is a timing table, not silicon: Wokwi's own cycle count
is a third opinion, and the firmware prints it.

**Not modelled:** translation (a gimbal, slide 6 — the station's race adds
it on the PC, slide 23b), battery sag, propeller
aerodynamics beyond linear thrust, frame flex. A real drone is harder.

## Slide 27: What Carries Forward

- **The contract.** Lesson 14 adds position: the same `sensors_t` grows a
  barometer and a position fix, and the attitude controller you tuned here
  becomes the inner loop of a position loop — a third level of cascade.
- **Measure before you optimise.** The plant cost more than the controller,
  and nobody would have guessed which line. A cycle count found it in one run.
- **The failsafe list.** Every robot in lessons 15–18 has its own version of
  "disarm when the estimate is absurd, when the sensor is stale, when the
  operator lets go" — and the same blind spot: it can only check what it
  can see.
- **Integrators wind, signs flip, loops buzz.** Lesson 17's balancing robot is
  this lesson's roll axis, standing on two wheels with gravity pulling it
  over — PID against LQR.
